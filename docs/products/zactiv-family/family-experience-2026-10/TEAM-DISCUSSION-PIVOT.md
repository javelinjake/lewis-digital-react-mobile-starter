# Continuing without Zack: a school-to-home business

Team discussion proposal · 2 October 2026

**Recommendation:** make the business about helping teachers teach movement and helping families enjoy it together. Use free, credible PE resources to earn trust; sell implementation support and optional family experiences; test a sponsor-funded route alongside direct family purchases. Build a contributor network so the offer does not depend on one personality.

“Family Moves” is used as a working name from the design exploration, not a checked or agreed trading name. This proposal assumes an initial England primary-school audience. Other nations need their own curriculum and funding checks. Prices, conversion rates and pilot thresholds below are hypotheses, not market benchmarks or forecasts. No live customer, financial or usage data was available for this analysis.

## 1. What changes when Zack leaves?

The business needs a clearer promise than access to a presenter’s videos:

> Confident PE lessons at school. Small, enjoyable moves at home.

The school product should be organised by year group, learning objective and lesson. The family product should answer “What could we do together in five minutes?” Contributors make the experience interesting, but the activity and its usefulness remain the reason to return.

Three possible directions:

| Direction | Main customer and benefit | Revenue | Main weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Teacher-first | Schools save preparation time and improve delivery | Resources, training, implementation | Can become a crowded school subscription business with a weak family link |
| Family-first | Parents find enjoyable, practical ways to move together | Activity packs and eventually membership | Distribution and willingness to pay are unproven |
| School-to-home | Children learn an activity in PE and invite their household to try it | School support, family packs, funded household access | Requires evidence that the bridge actually works |

Choose school-to-home as the initial experiment, using a very small family experience. Do not build three businesses simultaneously. A school may use the PE without promoting the app, and a family may use the app without belonging to a partner school.

Before relaunch: establish rights to the existing videos, scripts, lesson plans, music, likenesses and contributions. Confirm Darren/Teamspirit’s ownership and licence terms rather than assuming contributions belong to the business. Identify customer commitments and which content must be replaced. Agree a factual customer message, preserve working access and useful links, and make the next editorial owner explicit. Zack’s departure does not itself establish what can continue to be used.

## 2. Free PE: generosity needs a funding mechanism

| Model | What is free? | What pays for it? | When it makes sense |
|---|---|---|---|
| Free core, paid support | A usable set of reviewed lessons and essential PDFs | Teacher development, implementation and optional printed delivery | Best starting option if schools value help using the materials |
| Small annual school fee | Sample unit and home activities | Full resource access | Useful fallback if free adoption creates costs without revenue |
| Funded free access | Curriculum and family access within a defined programme | A sponsor, trust, employer or commissioned partner | Strong inclusion model, but contracts must cover delivery costs |

My preferred starting position: a free, complete pilot unit, then a published free core whose scope can be maintained. Expand towards a full curriculum only after checking rights, quality, coverage and operating cost. If the team can already fund a complete reviewed curriculum, free access is a credible differentiator; do not delay it just to create artificial scarcity.

Free must mean useful without paying. Keep essential teaching instructions, adaptations and safety information in the free lesson. Sell additional service, convenience or authored experiences. Avoid withholding the instructions necessary to deliver a lesson safely.

The current England PE and sport premium cannot fund core curriculum delivery. Eligible staff development and additional provision have distinct conditions; do not market a generic subscription or parent acquisition campaign as automatically eligible. [DfE guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/pe-and-sport-premium-for-primary-schools/pe-and-sport-premium-guidance-for-primary-schools)

The government has also announced a replacement partnerships network, planned to be fully operational from spring 2027. Treat this as a changing purchasing environment and investigate partnership opportunities rather than relying on historical school allocations. [DfE announcement](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/1bn-boost-to-pe-and-school-sport-to-end-fitness-postcode-lottery)

Two useful precedents: Premier League Primary Stars offers free resources and teacher training; GoNoodle has a free offer alongside paid SuperNoodle licensing, which can sometimes be underwritten by other organisations. These show possible structures, not evidence that our audience or prospective sponsors will behave the same way. [Primary Stars](https://plprimarystars.com/autumn22), [GoNoodle](https://support.gonoodle.com/article/192-is-gonoodle-really-free)

## 3. What the teacher actually gets

Make the unit of value a lesson teachers can confidently deliver tomorrow:

1. A clear learning objective and place in the sequence.
2. Equipment, space and setup diagram.
3. Warm-up, main activity and finish, with timings.
4. Two or three useful teaching cues and common misconceptions.
5. Ways to adapt the activity for different needs and available space.
6. A quick observation prompt to check learning.
7. An optional short demonstration clip.
8. A matching, equipment-light family activity.

Example: a throwing-and-catching lesson teaches a specific skill; the home card asks a household to practise passing a rolled-up pair of socks at a comfortable distance, with a seated option and a clear-space reminder. A PE educator must review the actual lesson and age suitability before publication.

**Yes to printable lesson plans.** Make them easy to read outdoors: one-page delivery card, setup picture, pupil activity cards where useful, and a simple progression/observation sheet. Provide free PDF downloads. Charge for optional printed and bound packs, replacement cards or facilitated implementation. Worksheets should support movement rather than displace it.

**Yes to PE videos that help teachers.** Prioritise 30–60-second demonstrations and two-minute teacher explanations of setup, teaching cues and adaptations. Teachers watch, pause and teach; pupils spend most of the lesson moving. Full follow-along videos can serve a particular warm-up or rainy-day purpose, but should not become the curriculum.

England’s curriculum includes a broader range of skills and experiences, including swimming and water safety. A collection of fitness routines is not a complete PE curriculum. Map what is covered and identify what schools still need to provide separately. [National curriculum PE](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/national-curriculum-in-england-physical-education-programmes-of-study/national-curriculum-in-england-physical-education-programmes-of-study)

## 4. Contributors: everyday role models, consistent quality

| Contributor | Example theme | School-to-home connection |
|---|---|---|
| Firefighter | Coordination, teamwork and everyday preparation | A reviewed family coordination game, without equipment or emergency simulation |
| Nurse or paramedic | Recovery and looking after ourselves | Gentle movement and a calm finish; avoid personalised medical advice |
| Police officer | Cooperation and clear communication | Follow-the-leader directions without crime, weapons or pursuit scenarios |
| Athlete or coach | A specific movement skill | A simplified home version with household equipment |
| Disabled athlete | Different ways to participate | Seated and standing choices presented as equally valid |
| Dancer | Rhythm and coordination | Make a three-move family dance |
| PE teacher | How to teach a skill well | A teacher demonstration plus an accessible home activity |
| Chef or food educator | Practical food skills | A separate adult-supported food activity, with allergy and equipment considerations |

Repeatable episode: a 20-second introduction, a short story about why movement matters to that person, a reviewed activity, and one invitation to try it together at home. A PE specialist designs or approves movement instruction; a respected profession is not a fitness qualification.

Use a standard brief, rights agreement, captions, framing guide, editorial review and institution permission where uniforms, logos or workplace affiliation appear. Pay or clearly agree contributor arrangements. Do not imply institutional endorsement without permission.

Film one activity in reusable pieces: teacher explanation, family demonstration, short introduction and still images for the PDF. Start with three contributors and measure whether their episodes increase actual activity attempts and returns. A large catalogue can wait.

## 5. The family product stays small

Home has one clear suggestion and two alternatives: move together, try a healthy everyday activity, or slow down. Most activities take 5–10 minutes, need little preparation and offer easier/seated alternatives where appropriate. A child can choose; an adult handles accounts and purchases.

An activity opens directly from a school link in a browser. Let the household try it before registration or installation. Afterwards, offer saving, a second idea or installation. Ask only for preferences that materially improve the next suggestion.

Keep basic activities, favourites and a simple personal history free. No broken streaks, guilt messages, calorie competition or public family rankings. An optional memory might say “You tried a new game together”; it should not imply health improvement or verify exercise from a tap.

Paid content is a clearly described set of additional experiences: a rainy-day collection, a weekend movement trail or a family dance pack. Show a sample and what equipment/time it needs. Offer it in an adult area after the household has experienced the free product’s value. Children should not be prompted to persuade adults to buy.

## 6. Twelve revenue ideas to discuss

These are alternative tests, not a launch checklist. Prices are starting hypotheses; account for taxes, payment/platform fees, refunds, support, production and fulfilment before setting them.

| Offer | Buyer and paid value | Example test | Priority |
|---|---|---|---|
| Family activity pack | Parent buys a specific useful collection | £5.99 Rainy-Day Moves; free sample and a clearly stated activity count | First |
| School implementation package | School buys onboarding, staff workshop and follow-up | £299–£499, fixed scope and hours | First |
| Funded local programme | Sponsor funds a school cluster and family experiences | £2,000–£5,000 six-week pilot, priced from actual delivery cost | First |
| Printed resource pack | School buys convenience and durability | Price from printing, packing, delivery and support costs | Early |
| PTA-funded family access | PTA buys a defined pack for every participating household | Simple annual or term agreement; no parent payment needed | Early |
| Multi-academy trust support | Trust buys consistent rollout across schools | Shared training and support priced by scope | Early after pilot |
| Family membership | Parent buys recurring, useful additions | Test £29.99/year or £3.99/month only after repeat demand | Later |
| Employer parent benefit | Employer funds staff households | Small adult opt-in pilot, employer sees aggregate uptake only | Later |
| Leisure-provider programme | Leisure centre funds useful activities and optional adult booking links | Local co-funded pack, transparent commercial relationship | Later |
| Community commission | Community/public partner funds participation outreach | Specific accessible programme and agreed aggregate reporting | Later; procurement can be slow |
| Facilitated family event | School/community pays for a movement session | One event introducing activities people can repeat at home | Selective; delivery-heavy |
| Resource licensing | Another organisation licenses reviewed content | Defined territory, usage, term and maintenance obligations | Later; rights and curriculum fit matter |

Avoid a child-directed advertising model, sale of household data, and an expensive content promise before recurring revenue exists. A sponsor funds a programme, not access to children’s attention or contact details. Parent-facing acknowledgement can be modest and explicit.

“Sponsor a school” needs a concrete buyer benefit: a reputable local participation programme, documented delivery and an honest aggregate report. Logo placement alone may not justify payment. Distinguish activities offered, families reached, self-reported participation and measured outcomes. Do not promise health improvement from usage data.

For a funded pilot, specify schools recruited, teacher support, materials supplied, family invitations, accessibility, evaluation and the sponsor’s permitted acknowledgement. State that uptake is voluntary. Define costs and delivery even if household uptake is lower than hoped.

## 7. How to get the app into families

The school-to-home bridge is a familiar activity, not a generic app advert.

| Technique | Concrete example | What to measure |
|---|---|---|
| Lesson-to-home card | “We tried this passing game in PE. Here is a five-minute home version.” | Visits and attempts per lesson card |
| Specific QR destination | QR opens the exact activity, not an app store or homepage | Landing-to-first-attempt rate |
| Teacher mention | “If you want to show someone at home, here is the card.” | Uptake versus card alone |
| Newsletter module | One activity, one image, one link, clear optional invitation | Visits and returning households |
| Existing parent channel | School shares an approved message through its normal channel | Channel-level aggregate conversion |
| Parent evening demonstration | Adult and child try an activity in two minutes | Follow-up attempts at home |
| Sports-day station | One inclusive game and a take-home card | Home use after the event |
| Child’s choice | Two suitable home ideas; child chooses which to share | Repeat use versus one fixed idea |
| Weekly optional invitation | A single timely idea, with consent and easy opt-out | Return rate and opt-outs |
| Rainy-day utility | Small-space, low-noise ideas when outdoor plans fall through | Repeat use in the relevant context |
| Holiday pack | Free activity first; optional larger collection | Pack purchases and later free use |
| Parent ambassadors | A few parents test usefulness and wording | Practical feedback and organic referrals |
| Community partner | Library or leisure centre shares a specific activity | Activated households by partner |
| Printed alternative | The activity is usable from the card without a phone | Feedback from households with limited access |
| Adult referral | Optional share link for an activity they enjoyed | Referred households that return |

Give teachers a ready-to-use communication kit and an honest explanation of free access and optional purchases. Offer a school-specific campaign link without collecting children’s names. Do not require app registration to complete school work or reward children for parent purchases.

The school should review its communications and data arrangements. Prefer school-distributed invitations and separate, explicit adult opt-in to our marketing rather than importing school parent lists. The ICO guidance requires transparent collection, an appropriate lawful basis and respect for marketing preferences. [ICO direct marketing guidance](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/direct-marketing-and-privacy-and-electronic-communications/direct-marketing-guidance/)

Suggested parent message for school review:

> This week children practised passing and catching in PE. If your household would enjoy trying a short home version, this optional activity takes about five minutes and uses a rolled-up pair of socks. It is free to try in your browser. Family Moves also offers optional paid collections; no purchase is needed for this activity.

Suggested sponsor proposition:

> Help us deliver a six-week school-to-home movement programme with a local school cluster. Your funding covers reviewed resources, teacher support and free household activities. We will report delivery and aggregate participation, with clear limits on what those measures tell us.

These are drafts for discussion, not messages sent to anyone.

## 8. The financial reality check

Illustrative funnel, deliberately using visible assumptions:

| Stage | Assumption | Result |
|---|---|---:|
| Schools | 20 | 20 |
| Unique invited households | 250 per school, after allowing for overlap | 5,000 |
| Visit | 30% of invited households | 1,500 |
| Try an activity | 40% of visitors | 600 |
| Return in the defined retention window | 30% of those who tried | 180 |
| Buy annual membership | 8% of returning households | About 14 |
| Annual gross receipts | 14 × £29.99 | £419.86 |

This is not a forecast. It shows why school distribution plus a parent subscription may be insufficient. Even strong reach can produce little revenue when the whole funnel is considered. Household overlap, genuine repeat use and annual renewal also matter.

For comparison, 20 schools buying a £300 support package generate £6,000 gross receipts, but workshops and support have real delivery costs. Two funded programmes at £3,000 also generate £6,000 gross, with their own costs and contractual obligations. Do not add these revenues together unless the buyers are paying for genuinely separate value.

Another simple test: if a pack costs £800 to produce, selling it for £5.99 requires 134 purchases merely to recover production in gross receipts, before any deductions or marketing/support costs. Cheap content is not automatically a profitable product.

For each experiment track contribution after variable costs, acquisition effort, support hours and repeat purchases/renewals. Use actual figures before deciding whether to offer full free curriculum indefinitely. Free access is a distribution choice, not evidence of a sustainable business model.

## 9. Three experiments instead of a large rebuild

**A. Does the bridge work?** Recruit 10 willing schools. Give them one reviewed six-week unit and matching family activities. Compare a specific activity invitation with a general app invitation across comparable school groups; with a small sample, treat results as directional. Record invitations actually distributed, unique visits, first attempts and households returning on a separate day. Interview both participants and non-participants. No extra child leaderboard or teacher data-entry burden.

**B. Does a parent pay for practical value?** Offer a finished, modest Rainy-Day Moves pack to adults after a useful free experience. Keep the free offer available. Measure purchases, actual pack use, refunds and whether buyers/free users return. Interview parents who declined. Do not treat checkout clicks or hypothetical willingness as sales evidence.

**C. Can someone else fund inclusion?** Prepare a costed school-cluster programme and talk to suitable local organisations, trusts or PTAs. Test a clear proposal and buying process. Distinguish polite interest, budget-holder engagement and a signed funded pilot. Sponsor conversations do not justify committing to free delivery before funding exists.

A fourth experiment can test a paid staff workshop, but only if delivery capacity permits. Hold the content scope steady while testing the buyer.

## 10. A practical 90-day plan

| Period | Work | Decision enabled |
|---|---|---|
| Days 1–14 | Rights/customer audit, choose initial age/unit, interview teachers and parents, agree pilot cost ceiling | Do we own the material and know the first useful problem? |
| Days 15–30 | Review the unit, make PDFs and three contributor pieces, simplify family entry, prepare measurement and communications | Is the offer usable enough to test? |
| Days 31–72 | Run the six-week school pilot; test one finished paid family pack and a costed funded programme | Do families return, and who actually pays? |
| Days 73–90 | Review costs, teacher feedback, cohort retention and paid evidence | Expand, change the invitation/offer, or stop the weakest route |

Assign an accountable owner for curriculum quality, family product, school relationships, revenue and measurement. One person can own several roles; responsibilities still need to be explicit.

Agree decision thresholds before launch. Example internal gates, to revise for pilot size: at least 6 of 10 schools actually deliver several lessons; at least 30% of households who try an activity return during days 8–14 after their first attempt; and one revenue route produces actual purchases or a contracted funded pilot at acceptable contribution. These are proposed management thresholds, not published benchmarks. Report denominators and small sample limitations.

If teachers use the resources but families do not activate, improve the school-to-home invitation and first activity before producing more videos. If families activate but do not return, improve everyday usefulness. If they return but do not pay, keep testing funded access or school support rather than locking the useful free core. If no buyer covers costs, reduce scope or reconsider the free commitment.

## 11. What the existing app needs before a broad pilot

The earlier source review found an account-led experience with separate workout, recipe, video and journal sections. The proposed first experience needs a direct activity entry, clearer age/space suitability and fewer choices. Existing school linking is a starting point, not a complete campaign attribution system.

Prioritise: guest-first activity access; adult-managed registration/purchases; a simple home suggestion; favourites; reliable activity loading and completion; basic aggregate campaign measurement; an honest free/paid boundary.

The previous audit also identified password-related registration data persisted in local storage and gaps in durable/offline activity writes. Address those before broad household recruitment. Reconfirm against the current code before implementing. Existing prototype actions and content are fictional and do not demonstrate live production reliability.

Defer social feeds, wearables, calorie tracking, complex scoring, open contributor uploads and a large family dashboard. The first business test does not require these.

## 12. Meeting decisions

Use a 60-minute discussion: 10 minutes on the promise and Zack transition; 15 on free scope and likely buyers; 15 on school-to-home acquisition; 10 on the three experiments; 10 on owners, cost ceiling and dates.

Leave with answers to five questions:

1. What can we legally and practically continue using?
2. What exactly stays free, and what maintenance can we fund?
3. Who is the first buyer, and what distinct value do they purchase?
4. What does the first household do within five minutes of opening the link?
5. What evidence will justify expanding after 90 days?

The family app is a promising candidate for the long-term product. The pilot must establish repeat usefulness and a viable payer; school registrations and video views alone cannot establish either.
