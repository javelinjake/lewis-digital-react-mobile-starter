# Original activity scripts and validation workbook

These are original editorial drafts, not prescribed exercise routines. Review age suitability, accessibility, safeguarding, allergies/choking risks for food activities and movement instructions with the appropriate content reviewers before publication. Keep adaptations specific to each script. Five minutes is a low-friction start, not a substitute for wider daily physical activity.

## A small catalogue with enough variety

Target 12 well-edited starter ideas; the studio implements seven plus a free pack preview. Every idea should have a title, realistic minutes, setting, equipment, three short steps, a seated/adapted option, adult guidance and a visible kind. Make and Chat remain family-time options without being counted as measured exercise.

| Idea | Fit | Opening invitation | Shared action | Adaptation / adult role |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Animal parade | Move · 5 min · indoors · no kit | “Can you move like your favourite animal?” | Child leads a gentle animal movement; everyone copies, then swaps. | Seated arm, hand and face movements. Adult checks clear space. |
| Mirror moves | Move · 5 min · indoors · no kit | “I'll be your mirror. What shape will you make?” | Slow arm shapes, swapping leader after a few moves. | Works seated; choose comfortable movements and distance. |
| Freeze-frame stories | Move · 10 min · indoors · no kit | “We're walking through an imaginary forest.” | Act the scene slowly; anyone calls freeze, then a new storyteller leads. | Hands/faces can tell the whole story. No jumping required. |
| Colour explorers | Move · 10 min · outdoors · no kit | “How many yellow things can we spot?” | Walk or roll a familiar accessible route; take turns choosing colours. | Outdoor seated spotting also valid as connection activity. Adult chooses/supervises route. |
| Tiny treasure route | Move · 10 min · indoors · familiar objects | “Let's point the way to three treasures.” | Adult sets three safe locations; children describe a route and the family walks/rolls it. | Arm gestures guide a seated explorer; no climbing or hidden hazards. |
| Slow-motion superheroes | Move · 5 min · indoors · no kit | “Everything happens in slow motion today.” | Reach, turn and gently stretch; each person invents a comfortable pose. | Seated arm versions; no combat, jumping or forceful stretching. |
| Shape makers | Move · 5 min · indoors · no kit | “Can we make a circle with our arms?” | Choose shapes and copy; invent a family shape together. | Hands or communication aids can choose/represent shapes. |
| Wind dancers | Move · 10 min · indoors · optional music | “What would a leaf do in the wind?” | Gentle movements grow and shrink; take turns leading. | Seated arm movement; sound optional and off by default. |
| Rainbow plates | Make · 20 min · indoors · familiar ingredients | “What colours are already in our kitchen?” | Children wash/arrange; adults prepare safe pieces; make a pattern together. | Comfortable tabletop height; adult checks allergies, choking risks, knives and heat. |
| Picnic helpers | Make · 10 min · indoors · available food | “Everyone gets one small picnic job.” | Arrange familiar pre-prepared snacks and choose a place to eat together. | Table or tray; adults handle preparation and supervise eating. |
| Pass the story | Chat · 5 min · indoors · no kit | “We found a tiny door...” | One sentence, sound or gesture each; choose an ending together. | Anyone can pass; allow signs/communication devices; no mandatory writing. |
| Cloud characters | Chat · 5 min · outdoors · no kit | “Who can you see up there?” | Describe a cloud, name it and imagine a short story. | Safe seated position; adult supervision, never look directly at the sun. |

Do not publish 100 superficially different ideas. Use variation inside the scripts: change the leader, story, colour or animal. Repetition can be useful; asking for novelty every day creates content expense and can undermine children who enjoy familiar games.

## A complete five-minute script

**Animal parade / content version 1 / intended draft audience 4–11 with an adult**

Card: “Animal parade — Take turns being the animal. Everyone else copies.” Metadata: “5 min · Indoors · No kit”. Start: “Let's play”.

Step 1: “Choose an animal”. One person picks an animal and shows its movement. Everyone copies at their own pace. Try a gentle bird or a stretching cat.

Step 2: “Swap your leader”. Let someone else choose. Make it as easy or silly as you like. You can show a shape, use a sound or say the animal's name.

Step 3: “Make your own parade”. Put two favourite movements together. Finish whenever your family is ready.

Seated version: “Make animal shapes with your arms, hands or face. Everyone can copy seated.” Adult guidance: “Find a clear space. Keep movements gentle; no jumping is needed.” Exit available at every step: “We tried it”.

Completion: “That was time well spent. You made a little moment together.” Feedback: “Again!” / “Not for us”. Optional note: “A little memory”. Exit: “Done for today”. Do not automatically ask for a recipe next.

## Concrete examples of adapting the existing catalogue

The live CMS catalogue was not inspected. The repository's E2E fixture includes a 30-minute Burpee Burnout Challenge; this demonstrates a possible workout presentation, not evidence that this is the real current home recommendation.

For any existing exercise workout, add a short parent-readable “Who this suits / space / equipment” summary. If it's not suitable for the target children, retain it in an adult-oriented section of Ideas rather than relabelling it as a family game. A reviewed five-minute “copy a leader” game can be new content linked alongside an existing workout; do not simply trim a prescribed workout without content review.

For an existing recipe, reuse ingredient checkboxes and cooking steps. Add one optional child role per suitable step: washing vegetables, arranging a plate, or stirring a cold mixture. Adults handle heat/knives. Surface total time and ingredients before suggesting it. Keep servings and instructions in the existing recipe flow; avoid another cooking subsystem.

For an existing journal prompt, let the family say the answer aloud and skip saving. “Pass the story” and “What made you smile?” can be short Chat ideas. Retain saved journal notes with a migration/export path. Do not turn a private note into a school progress report.

## Paid pack example: Space explorers

Six original missions with a reusable small story; one optional printable star chart. Suggested one-off price test £5.99. Free preview: Moonwalk mirrors, a five-minute seated or standing copy-the-leader game. Paid content should be materially authored and coherent, not just a paid recolour of free games.

| Mission | Shared activity | Production needs |
|---|---|---|
| Moonwalk mirrors (free preview) | Slow leader-copying shapes; arm versions seated. | One original illustration and three short steps. |
| Build your constellation | Make a family pattern with familiar safe objects on a table. | Printable star template; adult checks object suitability. |
| A signal from Mars | Take turns inventing gestures for a message; mirror or describe them. | Illustrated message cards; accessible text alternatives. |
| Comet explorers | Walk/roll a familiar route spotting shapes, or do the tabletop variant. | Separate route guidance and indoor version. |
| Mission picnic | Prepare and arrange familiar ingredients together. | Allergy/choking/heat guidance and adaptable ingredient examples. |
| Tell Earth our story | One sentence, sign or gesture each about the journey. | Short original story ending and optional memory prompt. |

Estimate pack cost before production: writing/editing, content review, illustration, print design, support and maintenance. Clips are optional. Purchase should not be needed to complete the free trail or view previously earned memories.

## Research before the build

Recruit 8–10 households for interviews, including people who signed up and stopped, current users and people who declined to use it. Include different child ages, household structures, access needs, available space and connectivity. Adult consent and an appropriate child assent process are required for research; minimise collected identifiers and do not record private family conversations by default. A suggested £20–£30 thank-you per household is a planning assumption.

Ask about a real recent evening, not their imaginary ideal app:

1. “Tell me about the last time you wanted to do something together but didn't.”
2. “What were you doing just before you opened Zactiv last time?”
3. “Show me where you would find something you could do right now.”
4. “What made you stop? What did you do instead?”
5. “Which games do the children ask to repeat?”
6. “Would this have helped that evening? What gets in the way?”
7. After showing a concrete pack: “What would you compare this purchase with? Which parts would you actually use?”

Avoid asking only “Do you like it?” or “Would gamification help?” Observe whether a family starts and how much reading/coordination it takes. Code the repeated barriers: relevance, effort, setup, trust, child interest, remembering, and technical failure. Keep observed evidence separate from inferred explanation.

## Observed prototype session: 20 minutes

Use 6–8 households and counterbalance current app/prototype order to reduce order effects. Do not tell them that the new design is expected to be better.

Task: “You have five minutes indoors before dinner. Find something you could do together.” Measure time until they select a suitable activity and the number of explanations needed. Then: “Someone wants to play seated”; “You stopped early”; “Find what your family did this week”. Ask the child which instruction they'd want to lead.

Compare trail with sticker memories, in alternating order. Ask “What does this tell you?” before explaining. A preference score alone cannot tell you whether either will improve repeat use. Watch for misunderstood markers, pressure from targets, or frustration at inaccessible instructions.

Practical proceed signal: most participants can choose/start unaided within 30 seconds and explain the family value. If more than two families struggle with the same step, fix it before the usage pilot. Those thresholds are working decisions for this small study, not statistical significance claims.

## Usage pilot and experiment sequence

Pilot 20–30 opt-in households over two weeks with 12 reviewed activities. Send one recruitment/instruction message through a channel you are authorised to use; this document does not send any messages. Offer help without repeated reminders. Ask for a short adult weekly check-in; participants can stop freely. Establish the baseline funnel first.

Run one substantial hypothesis at a time. A small pilot gives qualitative direction and exact counts; formal A/B testing would need a sample/power plan based on measured baseline differences.

| Hypothesis | Experiment | Main measure | Guardrail / stop condition |
|---|---|---|---|
| Registration blocks experiencing value. | Give public sample access before adult account creation; compare observed funnel with baseline. | Suitable activity start per eligible first-use session. | Account save must not lose guest progress; no public private-data access. |
| Parents need a useful default. | One curated card vs current category choices in observed tasks; roll out to pilot after refinement. | Time to start and later repeat attempts on separate days. | Swap reason shows relevance failures; fewer taps alone is insufficient. |
| Children want to lead. | Compare a neutral reviewed script with a child-choice/turn-taking script for the same action. | “Again” feedback, observed participation and requests to repeat. | Pressure or sibling conflict; allow passing/switching. |
| Shared progress is motivating. | Trail vs plain completed-history view; compare comprehension in sessions first. | Voluntary separate-day return and how parents describe the value. | No guilt, broken streak or desire to fake completion. |
| Forgetting is the main barrier. | Only after demand: one optional parent-selected reminder day/time. | Repeat attempts among opt-in households, plus interview reasons. | Opt-outs, complaints, prompt dependence and interruption. No escalating notification frequency. |
| Families will buy authored depth. | Show a finished pack preview to returning adults; then sell a pilot pack with working billing. | Real purchase per qualified preview; use after purchase. | Free repeat use and trust unchanged; no early access fee for earned progress. |

Do not infer causal effects from self-selected reminder users or compare different-age cohorts without accounting for the difference. Report “8 of 20 households returned” before reporting “40%”. Survey “easy to fit in” includes respondent count and response rate.

## Decision gates after the pilot

**Proceed:** enough households voluntarily return on separate days, the games fit their real circumstances, and observed technical save failures are resolved. Build the best-performing small loop, not every requested feature.

**Improve content:** starts occur but “Not for us”, age mismatch or repetition complaints dominate. Rewrite and review the first 12 ideas before adding functionality.

**Improve delivery:** families enjoy it but cannot find it in the evening. Test a school handout/deep link, a homescreen shortcut or one requested reminder. Do not add a social feed.

**Change audience/proposition:** repeated feedback says phones interrupt the desired activity, or adults don't value an ongoing app. Trial printable cards or institution-funded activity packs as the product rather than chasing app retention at any cost.

**Hold monetisation:** no repeated free use, no concrete purchase demand or cost exceeds a plausible contribution. A paid dashboard will not solve those problems.
